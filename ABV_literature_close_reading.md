# Close reading: latest Attention-Based View (ABV) literature

Source file: `combined_ABV_latest_literature_9de2.md` (four 2026 papers concatenated).

This note walks the file in intellectual order, not PDF order. The Journal of Management Studies (JMS) exchange is a Point–Counterpoint. Seidl, Splitter, and Whittington (2026) is the Point. Rhee (2026) and Nicolini, Hurst, Mengis, and Baeriswyl (2026) are the two Counterpoints. Kauppila and Vaara (2026) is a separate Academy of Management Review (AMR) theory paper on triadic joint attention in bottom-up strategy. It is cited by Seidl et al. as forthcoming and belongs in the same conversation.

---

## How the four papers fit together

Ocasio’s (1997) ABV is the shared starting point: firm behavior follows from what decision makers notice, encode, interpret, and focus time and effort on (issues and answers). Attention is structurally distributed through communication channels. The original theory inherited two Carnegie School premises: attention is scarce, and it is treated as a homogeneous resource that can be allocated more or less.

The 2026 debate is about whether those premises still hold in post-Chandlerian firms (porous structures, knowledge workers, AI, bottom-up strategy). Three positions:

1. **Seidl et al. (the Point).** Replace quantity with quality. Attention is not a limited homogeneous resource. It is *attending*: sociomaterial practices that are crescive (produced through doing) and differentiated by five constitutive qualities (sociality, discourse, embodiment, materiality, historicity).
2. **Rhee (Counterpoint 2).** Keep the ontology. Attention is scarce, selective, and situated. Practice insights can describe how the constraint is managed. They must not replace it. Without scarcity, attention explains everything and therefore nothing (blind spots, missed disruption, inattentional blindness).
3. **Nicolini et al. (Counterpoint 1).** Do not choose. Completing three “turns” inside ABV (allocation → competition; intentionality → thicker situations; presumed scarcity → questioned limits and possible abundance) is an *expansive* development, not a substitutive one. Quality and quantity mutually shape each other.

Kauppila and Vaara sit beside this debate. They keep ABV’s concern with limited attention and attentional engagement, but they replace the dyad (attendee–object) with a triad (attendee–coattendee–joint object). That is a microfoundation for how bottom-up initiatives become collectively attended and then reach the firm agenda.

## Paper 1. Seidl, Splitter, and Whittington (2026). Quality over Quantity of Attention! Towards a Qualitative Attention-Based View

**Outlet:** JMS Point. doi:10.1111/joms.70143. Authors: David Seidl (Zurich), Violetta Splitter and Richard Whittington (Oxford).

### Abstract and opening example

The paper proposes an *ontological* reorientation of ABV: from attention quantity to attention quality; from a resource to be rationed to sociomaterial practices of attending. Five constitutive qualities: sociality, discourse, embodiment, materiality, historicity. Attending is crescive and differentiated, not limited and homogeneous. This is claimed to fit contemporary strategizing and organizing.

The Ballmer vs Nadella example is the pedagogical core. Both CEOs spent long hours in meetings (similar quantity). Ballmer shouted and kept exchanges brief (“know it all”). Nadella listens with patience and empathy (“learn it all”). Leadership differs in *how* attention is done, not how much of it is spent.

### Why the shift: academic and managerial mandates

For researchers, quality matches the practice turn (Schatzki et al., 2001) already entering ABV (Brielmaier and Friesl, 2023; Kudesia and Lang, 2024; Nicolini and Korica, 2021; Nicolini and Mengis, 2024). Quality makes attention intrinsically variable by how it is done with others and with things.

For managers, quantity talk pushes overload, simplifying metrics, and remote styles. Knowledge-based firms need continuous dialogue and richer information (Grant, 2013; Ocasio et al., 2023a). Joseph et al. (2024) called the quantity/quality contrast “evocative but potentially quite misleading.” Seidl et al. reply that ignoring quality is what misleads.

The noun-to-verb move (Weick, 1969) is explicit: attending is something people *do*, not a pre-existing resource they allocate. Attending is *constituted of*—not merely shaped by—sociomaterial practices. The quarterly budget meeting example: heads turn to financial ratios on PowerPoint. What matters is not meeting length, talk volume, or headcount, but how attention is enacted together (bodies, IT, accounting practices, meeting conventions).

Quality here is *descriptive constitution*, not “better vs worse” (unlike Weick and Sutcliffe’s mindfulness, Rerup’s coherence/vividness/stability, or Splitter et al. 2024 on getting heard). Later they allow a secondary evaluative layer: practices have teloi and norms, so skilled performance can be more vivid, stable, coherent, or mindful.

### Two assumptions of the “quantitative ABV”

**Assumption 1: limited attention.** From Simon and Carnegie. The brain cannot process all stimuli. Attention is the bottleneck (Simon, 1973). Organizational structures (Chandler’s M-form, DuPont pyramid) ration attention by abstracting operations into financial ratios. Contemporary studies still treat attention as a capacity/resource analogous to capital (Eklund and Mannor, 2021; Eklund et al., 2025; Joseph et al., 2024).

**Assumption 2: homogeneous attention.** Intensity, breadth, and vigilance are degrees of the same thing. Mindfulness in Ocasio (1997) is active focusing (selection), not a different kind of attention. Vigilance in Ocasio (2011) is high vs low sustained attention. Empirical work counts words in earnings calls, chairman letters, or forum replies (Eklund and Mannor, 2021; Eklund et al., 2025; Rhee et al., 2019; Haas et al., 2015; Rhee, 2024; Chen et al., 2015; Ahn, 2020). The authors stress that “quantitative ABV” is ontological, not methodological: Joseph and Ocasio’s (2012) GE case is qualitative method with a quantitative ontology.

### Challenges to quantity already inside ABV

Post-Chandlerian firms (AI, dynamic processes, knowledge workers) make detached rationing inadequate (Ocasio et al., 2023b; Love, 2024; Ocasio, 2025). Ocasio et al. (2018) added communication inside “pipes and prisms.” Dialogue is generative, not cheese-paring. Weick and Sutcliffe (2006) already argued quality (mindfulness as rich awareness of discriminatory detail) can make attention more plentiful when disciplined. Rerup (2009) adds coherence and “elasticity” of capacity. Seidl et al. go further: attending is crescive, not merely elastic.

### From attention to attending

Practice theory treats practices, not individuals, as the unit of analysis. Reckwitz (2002): practices combine bodily and mental activities, things, know-how, emotion, motivation. Nicolini and Korica (2021) already say attention is something managers do, but they still treat practices as activating/guiding *cognitive* attention. Seidl et al. go further: attention *only exists as* the performance of attentional practices. “Attention is nothing but the performance of attentional practices.”

Redefined attending: a set of practices (purposefully or purposively) aimed at noticing, encoding, interpreting, and *structuring* (they replace Ocasio’s “focussing”) of issues and answers; recurrent bodily activities performed with others and with things. Four points: (1) recurrent patterns, not idiosyncrasy; (2) a set of practices, not one; (3) telos may be intended or merely purposive (Chia and Holt, 2006); (4) materiality, bodies, others.

Illustration throughout: Splitter et al. (2024) Open Strategy ethnography. CEO and frontline employees present issues, coach framing, work in project groups. When the CEO texts during a meeting, presenters pause and energy flags—same headcount, attention evaporates. That example is later used by Rhee as evidence that scarcity is still doing the work.

Two implications of the practice move:

1. **Against pre-given limits.** Learning improves attending. Employees learn to encode; the CEO learns to notice. “The more attending you do the more attention you have.” Crescive, not depletive.
2. **Against homogeneity.** Noticing ≠ encoding ≠ debating. PowerPoint presentation ≠ dialogic debate. Comparing them as “more/less attention” is apples and pears. Strategy depends on *how* people attend, not only how much.

### Five constitutive qualities

**Sociality.** Interactions are not context around attention; they *are* attending. Hierarchical actors often set how lower-level attending is done (Nicolini and Korica, 2021). Joint attention depends on who participates (they cite Kauppila and Vaara, forthcoming).

**Discourse.** Communication is not a “shaper” of attention (contra Ocasio et al., 2018; Brielmaier and Friesl, 2023). It is part of doing attention. Unit vocabulary vs corporate vocabulary in Splitter et al. (2024): integration required identity change (operational specialist → corporate strategist), not just a cognitive switch. Discourses are internalized, not tools picked up and dropped.

**Materiality.** PowerPoint vs Post-its constitute different attending (iterative recombination vs unidirectional presentation). Algorithms and ML can create attention rules without full human awareness (Nicolini and Mengis, 2024). Conversational AI may hybridize strategists’ internal dialogue (Whittington, 2025). Technologies are inside cognition, not merely around it.

**Embodiment.** Gaze, raised hand, posture produce different kinds of attention (interrupt, notice, redirect). Bodies are disciplined and prefigure attending (Nicolini and Mengis, 2024). Joseph et al. (2024) on facial/body recognition is acknowledged but treated as still too emotion-as-add-on rather than attending-as-bodily-doing.

**Historicity.** Not just clocks and meeting schedules (Brielmaier and Friesl, 2023). Each performance leaves Giddensian “memory traces.” Practices have a social life and collective memory (Nicolini and Mengis, 2024). Sequencing matters: generic coaching before specific coaching in Splitter et al. (2024). The same practice does not have the same effect at different times. Learning enlarges repertoire and skill.

These five combine. Attending can be exclusive or inclusive; jargon-laden or not; in-person or remote; PowerPoint or Post-it; first-time or routine.

Table I contrast:

| Quantitative ABV | Qualitative ABV |
|---|---|
| Attention | Attending |
| Allocation | Performance |
| Limited | Crescive |
| Homogeneous | Differentiated |
| Individuals, cognition, structure, external situatedness | Practices constituted of the five qualities |
| Indicative: Ocasio 1997; Rhee 2024; Eklund et al. 2025 | Indicative: Nicolini and Korica 2021; Nicolini and Mengis 2024; Splitter et al. 2024 |

### Research implications

Two agendas. First, revisit quantitative findings: pipes and prisms without practices inside them are incomplete; Chandlerian structures may diminish attending while participative ones may enhance it through learning. Second, study novel assemblages of the five qualities, and why practices are hard to drop (identity). Cross-fertilize with Strategy as Practice.

Closing claim: quality has primacy. The quantitative ABV’s insights risk distracting from the social nature of attention. Shift from the negative of overload to the positive of learning.

## Paper 2. Rhee (2026). Attention Is Attention: Preserving Ontological Integrity in Organizational Research

**Outlet:** JMS Counterpoint. doi:10.1111/joms.70140. Author: Luke Rhee (UC Irvine).

### Abstract and stance

Rhee’s title is the thesis: attention is attention. Inspired by ABV, organizational research has shown how firms learn, adapt, and perform. Recent theorizing (the Point) risks diluting attention’s explanatory force by compromising ontological integrity. Attention must remain a scarce, selective, situated mechanism linking cognition, structure, and behaviour. Re-ontologizing attention as crescive collapses causal explanation into phenomenological description. Persistent gaps are misdiagnosed as ABV limits; they actually come from under-using the ontology already in the definition. Two gaps: weak normative theorizing; thin theorization of situations. Three agendas: attention patterns that manage scarcity; costs of selective attention; information environments that condition returns on attention.

### Why scarcity is not optional

The enduring ABV insight: external and internal conditions shape the distribution of attention, and patterned attention drives behaviour and performance. Foundational assumption: attention is scarce (March, 1962; Simon, 1976). Managers cannot attend to everything; they may improve existing products rather than scan new markets. Information volume, speed, and fragmentation make scarcity *more* defining, not less. Ocasio (2024/2025): attention is a potent mediator across contexts and levels.

Seidl et al. say attention is enacted, socially/materially/discursively constituted. Rhee grants that this may describe everyday unfolding. It does not change ontology. Practices themselves compete for limited attention. Within practices, actors still narrow focus at others’ expense. Practice insights add texture; they do not make attention expandable without cost.

The concern is *inadequacy* (Locke and Golden-Biddle, 1997), not irrelevance of practice theory. Removing scarcity and replacing it with crescion undermines the engine that made attention powerful. Without scarcity one cannot explain blind spots, missed disruption, overlooked peripheral competitors, or missed weak signals (Christensen, 1997; Henderson and Clark, 1990; Rerup, 2009; Vuori and Huy, 2016). Those failures are predictable consequences of scarcity. A crescive ontology cannot say why they persist.

Practice, communication, and socio-material perspectives can be absorbed as *contextual factors*: attention-shaping architectures and attention-contesting arenas. They show how the constraint is managed without dissolving it.

### The quality/quantity label is a misdirection

Seidl et al. are right that the disagreement is ontological, not methodological. They are wrong to label it qualitative vs quantitative. That invites readers to hear a method choice. The actual stake is whether scarcity remains constitutive. Scarcity ontology does not require quantitative methods. Some of the strongest scarcity-tradition work is qualitative (Joseph and Ocasio, 2012; Rerup, 2009; Vuori and Huy, 2016). Qualitative methods may be essential for patterns over time, real-time tradeoffs, and situational returns.

Once scarcity is abandoned, expansion displaces precision. A world without limits does not resemble managerial life. Danger is not only dilution but *dissolution*: attention becomes coextensive with practice; a concept that explains everything explains nothing. “In an era defined by the accelerating poverty of attention, the field cannot afford this indulgence.”

### Ontology of attention (what the definition already implies)

Ontology: what exists and what causal work an entity can do (Bhaskar, 1978; Searle, 1995). Definition (Ocasio, 1997, p. 189): noticing, encoding, interpreting, and focusing of time and effort on issues and answers. Three attributes follow as “absolute laws of its essence”:

**Scarcity.** Time and effort are finite. Scarcity is not a fixed quantum and does not deny that capacity can grow. An actor who focuses intensely on a critical issue may deepen attention to it—but any gain is purchased by attending to less elsewhere. Tradeoff, not a fixed stock. Cognitive limits make this constitutive, not a contingent environmental problem like information overload. Scarcity is an ontological limit on which information can even enter consciousness.

**Selectivity.** The principle by which scarcity is resolved: exclusion through which a reality becomes available (James, 1890). Distinct from the organizational processes that specify *which* issues get selected (Weick’s bracketing; Prahalad and Bettis’s dominant logic; Kaplan’s framing contests). Those processes determine content; selectivity is the fact that such processes must exist. Selectivity makes environments cognitively manageable and simultaneously leaves much of reality outside attention.

**Situatedness.** Attention exists only in a situation. Not a general-purpose capacity applied uniformly. Distinct from selectivity: the driver at an intersection cannot see several blocks away (situatedness); within the visible range, they still choose the light over the billboard (selectivity). Organizations construct situations via roles, tasks, meetings, technologies. Issues outside an actor’s role/forum/interaction cannot enter attention then, regardless of objective importance (Goffman, 1959; Hutchins, 1995).

These three already quietly underwrite JMS ABV papers (McMullen et al., 2009; Washburn and Bromiley, 2012; Mazzelli et al., 2019; Junge et al., 2023; Ademi et al., 2025). The Point pushes the opposite direction.

### Why losing integrity is costly (three reasons the Point fails Rhee’s test)

Ontological replacement is warranted only if it explains phenomena the old ontology could not, without losing what the old ontology already explained well. Rhee finds the Point counterproductive.

He splits the Point into two moves and accepts only the first:

1. **Differentiation** (attention is not homogeneous; how it is done matters). Already present in ABV empirics (Eklund and Mannor, 2021; Rhee, 2024). Absorbable.
2. **Crescion** (attention is produced and expanded through practice). This is the integrity-compromising move.

**Reason 1.** Crescion removes the constraint that does the explanatory work and is silent on cost. Learning-based capacity growth is real, but growth is reallocation: skill grows only in practices one invests in. Expansion operates *within* the tradeoff scarcity names. Crescive attention is inherently narrowing (Simons and Chabris gorilla). Crescive processes gain momentum without reflexive awareness of exclusion—“selectively unconscious.” That is why firms miss disruption. A theory that tracks accumulation without displacement cannot explain blindness.

**Reason 2.** The framework collapses attention into its situation. If context is constitutive of attention rather than a condition acting on it, situation boundaries become indeterminate. Counterfactuals about roles, forums, and arrangements lose a stable situation distinct from the attention it shapes.

**Reason 3.** Category error: mistaking the mode of expression for the mechanism of selection. ABV already had attentional engagement as socio-cognitive (Ocasio, 2011) and communication/routines (Ocasio et al., 2018) without negating scarcity. TMT example: CFO and CTO extend each other’s reach, but the team still has a finite agenda. Recast as crescive, richer dialogue “produces” more attention—but cannot answer why some agendas enter consciousness and others do not. Tautology risk: infer high-quality attention from successful outcomes.

Even the Point’s own examples presuppose scarcity: CEO texting is reallocation of scarce time/effort; discursive strategy-making competes with material data review in the same window.

Post-Chandlerian conditions (dispersed knowledge, AI) *intensify* scarcity: the gap between what could be attended and what can be attended widens. The contemporary case strengthens the scarcity ontology; it does not retire it.

### Direct reply to Nicolini et al.

Nicolini et al. treat scarcity as Simon’s contingent legacy, to be loosened so attention can expand through collective and socio-technical means. Rhee: scarcity is not one theorist’s inheritance. It is independently established (Kahneman, 1973, attention as effort on a finite pool; Fiske and Taylor, 2013, cognitive miser). Filing scarcity under Simon mistakes a formulation for the source. Variation in *degree* of scarcity across situations presupposes the constraint. Treating scarcity as one situational possibility among others sets aside the constitutive constraint from which explanatory leverage derives.

Ordering of explanation: practices, discourse, and material infrastructures shape how scarce/selective/situated attention is organized. They do not replace attention as the object of explanation. Joseph et al. (2024) already embrace interactional, relational, temporal dynamics while preserving the scarcity core.

### Two gaps from insufficient ontological commitment

**Gap 1: insufficient normative theorizing.** ABV has been largely descriptive (which issues get attention; what follows). Missing: how attention *ought* to be selected when tradeoffs are unavoidable. Simon paired poverty of attention with the claim that organizations must allocate deliberately. Streams already treat attention as competed-over (Bouquet and Birkinshaw, 2008; Dutton and Ashford, 1993; Haas et al., 2015; Hansen and Haas, 2001; Piezunka and Dahlander, 2015) but have not been raised into an explicit normative agenda. Every act of attending carries opportunity cost.

**Gap 2: situations conflated with formal structure.** Situatedness is operationalized via hierarchies, roles, divisional forms. Structure generates heterogeneous situations that can vary within the same design and change faster than formal design (Puranam, 2018; Csaszar and Rhee, 2026). Adjacent evidence: networks (Rhee and Leonardi, 2018, 2024); shared emotions (Vuori and Huy, 2016); socio-material prefiguration (Nicolini and Mengis, 2024). Need: situations as causal objects in their own right.

### Three research agendas

**Agenda 1: handling scarcity through attention patterns.** Outcomes depend on how attention is directed and sustained, not only how much. Weick and Sutcliffe (2006): attention is scarce when undisciplined, more plentiful when stable. Rhee (2024) CEO attentional vigilance: stable attention over time protects exploration without expanding capacity; it redistributes across a longer horizon. Other patterns: executive control, vigilance, episodic engagement; broad vs narrow (Eklund and Mannor, 2021); cognitive flexibility (Laureiro-Martínez and Brusoni, 2018); construal shifts (Wiesenfeld et al., 2017). Practice theory can describe socio-material texture of patterns (standing meetings vs embodied disruptions) without becoming ontology. This agenda is normative in a theoretical sense: how attention should be allocated over time under constraint.

**Agenda 2: attention tradeoffs and the cost of attention.** Selectivity as cost. Gains are financed by losses. Structured, not uniform, displacement. Exploration–exploitation is canonical (March, 1991; Benner and Tushman, 2002; O’Reilly and Tushman, 2013). Rhee (2024): CEO exploration and exploitation attention negatively correlated. Rhee and Leonardi (2024): attention to non-brokers crowds out brokers. Map substitutes, complements, independents across the issue–answer space. Practice qualities (Seidl’s five) can moderate tradeoff structure (slide-deck meetings vs interactive format; financial-efficiency vocabulary rendering exploration invisible) without replacing scarcity. Practical payoff: safeguards, compensatory structures, sequencing.

**Agenda 3: designing information environments to improve returns on attention.** When patterns are hard to change, situatedness is the lever. Information environments (choice architectures, filters, aggregation) are return multipliers. Knowledge markets (Hansen and Haas, 2001; Haas et al., 2015); crowding (Piezunka and Dahlander, 2015). Key exception: Rhee and Leonardi (2018)—focused vs divided attention pays off differently in constrained vs unconstrained networks; patterns stay constant, situation changes. Practice theory operationalizes environments (dashboards vs reports vs briefings; historicity of arrangements). Contingency theory: when vigilance compensates for volatility, when divided attention amplifies signal vs noise. Normative insight is conditional, not universal.

### Conclusion and footnote

“Attention is attention.” Preserve scarcity, selectivity, situatedness. The three agendas exploit the ontology rather than expand it. Practice insights enter as descriptions of constraint management. Footnote 1: a firm that misses a threat is explained by selective absorption of existing priorities, not by narrating meetings and reports. Without the ontological anchor one can describe organizational life richly but cannot explain why the threat went unseen.

## Paper 3. Nicolini, Hurst, Mengis, and Baeriswyl (2026). Beyond Quality and Quantity: Enriching the Study of Organizational Attention

**Outlet:** JMS Counterpoint. doi:10.1111/joms.70141. Authors: Davide Nicolini (Warwick; BI Oslo), Matthew Hurst, Jeanne Mengis (Università della Svizzera italiana), Manuel Baeriswyl.

### Abstract and dialectical positioning

This Counterpoint wants an *expansive* rather than *substitutive* development of ABV. Endorses a richer understanding of attention. Warns against quality/quantity dichotomies. Perceived ABV limits (scarce, quantifiable attention) reflect Simon’s legacy more than flaws in ABV itself. Three future directions: (1) resource allocation as emerging from competition among attentional sources; (2) thicker situatedness in which social dynamics shape attention beyond intentionality; (3) revisit scarcity—why limited, what kind of scarcity, when abundance not scarcity is the problem. Dialectical synthesis: transcends the Point’s complete turn to quality and Rhee’s “stick to quantity and persevere in normal science.”

Keywords include practice theory and situated attention. The authors note they were among the first to advocate a practice-based perspective that complements ABV (Nicolini and Korica, 2021; Nicolini and Mengis, 2024).

### Introduction: hold quality and quantity in tension

They share the Point’s interest in a differentiated, qualitatively rich view, and share Rhee’s concern with how actors manage attentional phenomena in practice. They reject unhelpful dichotomies (quantitative vs qualitative analysis). Many ABV studies are variance studies that quantify attention, but that is because many authors still follow “Simon’s legacy,” which pre-dates ABV.

They agree with overcoming “attention as a pre-given limited resource” but deny that the way to do this is to brand the entire ABV as necessarily quantitative. Substituting one restrictive view with another—“attention is nothing but the performance of attentional practices”—is unhelpful. ABV already has attentional engagement (Ocasio, 2011). Focusing on how attention is produced in attending solves some issues, not all. The Point “says little about the actual quality of attention”: the five constituent qualities are about the *nature of attending*, not the quality of attention.

ABV is not a unified theory but a “programmatic theoretical approach” (Joseph et al., 2024, p. 7). Their proposals expand it rather than reject it.

Roadmap: challenge Simon; show how his legacy biased ABV; three ways to overcome it. “Without throwing the baby out with the bathwater.”

### Simon’s legacy (the hidden driver of the quality–quantity debate)

ABV emerged at the intersection of Simon (1947)/Carnegie, Cyert and March (1963), and Weick (1979) (Joseph et al., 2024; Ocasio, 2025; Ocasio and Love, 2026). From Simon: decision making is central; attention allocation to problems and opportunities; cognitive limits; structure as attention-directing; structural decomposability.

Less well known: Simon’s information-processing framing answered a specific historical problem—large bureaucracies drowning in memos and reports. Specific role: executives in large government organizations. “Human beings, like contemporary computers, are essentially serial devices. They can attend to only one thing at a time” (Simon, 1971, p. 41). Measurement: time spent on a physical message (in-tray, secretary). Instant global digital messaging was inconceivable. Davenport and Beck’s (2001) attention-as-market is a later overlay, not Simon.

Some ABV authors have tamed their “inner Simon” via attentional engagement: intentional, sustained focus of cognitive resources for problem solving, planning, sensemaking, decision making (Ocasio, 2011). This moderates Simon’s determinism (attention descending from structure/authority) and makes room for agency. Engagement is situated in time, place, and channels; connections among communicatively situated events determine organizational attention. Better fit for post-Chandlerian firms (Ocasio et al., 2023). Also: language/discourse as medium of attentional control (Ocasio et al., 2018); technologies structuring attention (Ocasio, 2025). The Point, they argue, does not adequately credit this.

But Simon still constrains ABV in three ways (Table I):

1. **Top-down allocation/control.** Framing as how controls affect allocation in channels. Many studies downplay situated variation and keep a control focus (Brielmaier et al., 2025; Love, 2024). Unintended top-down bias: TMTs as attention-setters. Politics, regulation, and other concurrent attention-setting processes are ignored (Nicolini and Korica, 2021).
2. **Intentionality.** Directing attention and joint attention treated as largely intentional though context-dependent. Conflicts with Weick (1995): action often retrospectively portrayed as intentional. Weak situatedness. Managers know meetings where attention went far from where it was intended.
3. **Time scarcity as the whole story.** Where does scarcity come from? Is attention only information processing? Over-emphasis looks incongruous to managers building business models on apparent abundance, augmentation, automation, AI delegation. For them scarcity is not the issue; abundances are.

Table I maps, for each turn: Simon’s assumption; quantitative view (Rhee); quality response (Point); emerging ABV turn; how to complete the turn.

### Turn 1: from top-down allocation and control to competition

ABV explains allocation by identifying factors that “control” it. Scarcity and a central allocating mechanism travel together.

Instead of replacing cognitive processes with practices (Point), turn to neurophysiology: individual attention emerges from competition among concurrent perceptual processes, not central allocation (Bruineberg, 2025). Biased competition (Desimone and Duncan, 1995; Fernandez-Duque and Johnson, 2002): stimuli compete for neural representation; attentional signals bias toward task-relevant or salient information via bottom-up (loud, bright, novel, sudden) and top-down (goals, expectations, knowledge, norms, emotions) mechanisms (Beck and Kastner, 2009).

This keeps cognition–structure–behaviour linkages (Rhee) without rejecting cognition for practices alone (Seidl). Current control emphasis: “cold” cognitivist character; ignores “warm” feelings/emotions often driving who wins neural competition. Managers know hunches. Language is still symbol manipulation à la Simon.

Collectively, control grants managers undue importance, weakening ABV’s distributed aspiration. Most empirical ABV studies focus on TMT/upper echelons (Brielmaier and Friesl, 2023b). Cybernetic language (“attention regulators,” “channels”) invites accusations of structuralist determinism that the Point levels at ABV.

A competitive/negotiated view is closer to managerial reality. Multiple attentional sources: taste (Hennion), habitus (Bourdieu), attentional styles (Zerubavel), persuasion, seduction, aspiration, hope. Other organizational mechanisms: communities of thought (Zerubavel); attentional regimes in digital economy (Citton); attentional meta-practices (Kudesia; Kudesia and Lang); socio-technical nexuses (social media). New units of analysis would allow studying attentionality as political—mentioned by Ocasio but rarely studied empirically because Simon framing pushes correlations between controlling factors and outcomes.

Bottom-up processes: issues represented as “urgent” (Sullivan, 2010); early ideation (Di Vincenzo et al., 2021). Political economy of attention and technologies of attention (Franck; Pedersen et al.; van Krieken) have barely entered ABV.

This would help study post-Chandlerian fluid organizing (social movements, entrepreneurial ecosystems) without stretching “attentional structure” and “regulators” designed for industrial bureaucracies. Corporate attention-control systems become *one actant* competing in an attentional arena, not the sole actant. The Point leaves power and politics unaddressed.

Inattention becomes first-class. Paying attention to one thing entails disregarding another. Inattention can be non-deliberate overlooking of the socially unmarked (Brekhus) or post-perceptual disregard. Configurations of deliberate/non-deliberate attention and inattention are the politics of attention. Practically, blind spots matter as much as operating attentional controls. Rhee’s tradeoff agenda is acknowledged as partial.

ABV already has competition elements: Ocasio’s (2012) conditioned garbage can (meetings where priorities compete); Brielmaier and Friesl (2023a) attention contests in open strategy—but contests are not studied in depth. Expanding toward a coherent competition model has more explanatory power than moving from nouns to verbs.

### Turn 2: from intentionality to thicker situations

Ocasio’s attentional engagement definition is intentional allocation. This gives an intellectualist tone and over-emphasizes managers as strategy setters. The Point lets intentionality disappear into practices; Rhee reasserts it via selectivity as ontology.

Ocasio (2025) claims debt to Weick: if one understands response repertoires and how attention is controlled by them, a better theory of organizational behaviour follows. Nicolini et al.: Weick’s point is that members often do *not* think before acting in Simon’s sense. “How do I know what I paid attention to until I see what I did?” Intentional attention is the exception. Principle “where attention goes, decision follows” can reverse: attention follows from decisions. Ingold: every movement is a movement of attention.

ABV’s most novel idea is situated attention in communication channels (Brielmaier and Friesl, 2023b), but the view of situation is thin: attention varies by time and place like garbage-can ebbs and flows, or enumerating regulators (rules, position, resources, routines, players, logics). How factors play out is underexplored. Framing as “what affects situational attention” orients scholars toward variance studies. Rhee also notes insufficient theorizing of situations vs formal structure and wants situations as causal objects. Nicolini et al. want situations as *agential* (Goffman, 1964), not stages for intentional decision making.

Structure shapes settings, but interaction orients and co-orients attention: temporary salience (LinkedIn exposure, Brielmaier et al., 2025); social roles/performances (popular or angry person in the room); who is in the channel (CEO as centre, Splitter et al., 2024); words vs tone vs status. Goffman studied attention in his dissertation. Situations can shift mid-stream. Situations are units of analysis in which attention structures are “inhabited.”

Joint attention: Ocasio (2025) draws on Tomasello. The Point omits psychologically derived joint attention for an exclusive focus on practices. ABV often uses an additive view: individuals converge because of shared regulators (Berchicci and Tarakci, 2022; Zhang et al., 2022). Instead, joint attention is co-creation of intentionality by merging, crafting, scaling attentional resources. Kidwell and Zimmerman (2007): joint attention is interactional—attending to each other (mutual attention) as much as to objects. Shared representations are not even a prerequisite for joint action (Sebanz et al., 2006). Situations are not containers of regulators; joint attention *emerges through* situations. Emotionally driven local joint foci may not amount to collective intentionality.

Broader context links: situational awareness (Endsley, 1995; Bedny and Meister, 1999); attention-economy switching costs (Citton, 2017); algorithms as more than a new control mechanism (Ocasio, 2025)—human–AI collaboration and algorithmic management (Hillebrand et al., 2025).

### Turn 3: attention is limited, but is it always “scarce,” and why?

The Point abandons scarcity; Rhee reasserts it as an ontological law. Nicolini et al.: Simon’s information-processing framing is generative (prediction, comparison, causal explanation, as Rhee says) but increasingly constraining.

Fixed-amount attention poorly matches individual differences (Gruszka et al., 2010: control, flexibility, working memory, mood, traits) and dynamic quality: waxing and waning, boredom vs captivation, boostable when needed (Bruineberg, 2025). Limits exist; they are not always fixed, and limitation is not inherently problematic. They *disagree* with any suggestion that attention is limitless (Sharpe and Tyndall, 2025; Styles, 2006). Better questions: when are limits experienced as scarce and problematic; when as abundant; how do the two dynamize.

Evidence of abundance-like expansion: synchronous coattention / shared attention improves recall and polarizes judgments (Shteynberg and colleagues). Collective attending is not sequential information processing. Attentional foci are dynamically re-established; people can co-present without shared attention (Bergmann and Peräkylä, 2024). Scarcity can have positive and negative effects (Goldsmith et al., 2020). Situated attending beyond deficiency views (Eklund et al., 2025). Synchronous co-attending temporarily extends limits.

Material/technological/cultural mediation: technology both consumes and expands attention. Attention-economy authors argue that “new tech depletes attention” obscures augmentation and automation. Routines (monthly reviews, annual planning) require less deliberate attention, expanding capacity (Nicolini and Korica, 2021). Immersive tech and remixing enable expanded learning. Human–AI enmeshment includes reflexive attending across actual and digital environments (Elliott, 2019). Socio-technical and cultural attentional scaffolding manages and expands constraints.

Quality and quantity are mutually related, not ontologically opposed:

- **Quality shapes quantity.** Cafeteria visits as unscheduled-but-routine encounters activate and expand attention (Nicolini and Korica, 2021). High-reliability organizations: respectful interaction / heedful interrelating resist simplification *and* keep “everything in sight all at once” (tower, multiple screens)—practices oriented toward expanding limits, a quantity concern.
- **Quantity shapes quality.** Information exceeding capacity lowers quality (Dellavigna and Pollet, 2009); rational inattention (Sims, 2010). Football talent study (Baeriswyl et al., 2026): organizations structurally separate future potential from current performance, but in practice attend to potential *through* performance because the latter is familiar and measurable. Quantity of informational/task demands plus goal conflicts is not solved by distributing specialized attention across units (Joseph and Wilson, 2018); the solution reduces talent to performance. Problematic quantity shapes quality.

### Conclusion: managers and MOS

Expansive moves broaden ABV beyond strategic decision making to other forms of organizing: more multi-faceted, conflictual, process-oriented. Moving from Simon’s legacy allows exploring assumptions beyond a fixed theoretical framework (Cornelissen, 2025). They even play on words: ABV fully becoming an “Activity-Based view of the Organization.”

Managerial resonance: attention emerges from competition among people, things, conventions, discourses. Even CEOs’ attention is often “mortgaged” (Nicolini and Korica, 2021); they must carve out discretion. Attentional conventions, routines, cultural expectations produce built-in blind spots. Manage attention dynamics in meetings rather than be managed by them. Consider how industry/organization/process attributes render attention scarce, and how digital technologies enable abundances, augmentations, automations, and new business models not predicated on presumed scarcity.

## Paper 4. Kauppila and Vaara (2026). Triadic Attentional Processing and Buildup of Joint Attentional Engagement in Emergent Strategic Initiatives

**Outlet:** Academy of Management Review. doi:10.5465/amr.2024.0120. Authors: Olli-Pekka Kauppila (Hanken), Eero Vaara (Oxford). Editor: Anastasiya Zavyalova.

### Core claim

Traditional ABV is dyadic: attendee attends to object (“I attend to X”). That explains top-down distribution and enactment of attention, and behavioural focus, but not how attention emerges through bottom-up strategic initiatives. They introduce a triadic approach: attendee, coattendee, joint object (“I and another person jointly attend to X”). Drawing on social cognition and social agency, a three-phase process model explains the buildup of joint attentional engagement (Ocasio’s “sustained allocation of cognitive resources” on joint objects).

The paper is theory-building with numbered propositions. OCR of tables is badly garbled; the prose is recoverable.

### Why this is needed

ABV acknowledges top-down and bottom-up influences (Ocasio, 2011; Ocasio and Joseph, 2005) but most work is top-down. That is inadequate for porous, flat, post-Chandlerian firms where strategy often emerges from autonomous initiatives (Burgelman, 1983; MacKay et al., 2021; Mirabeau and Maguire, 2014). Recent ABV work on upward communication, bottom-up influence on channels, and TMT attention to signals still lacks a robust account of how micro-interactions coalesce into collective attention to emergent initiatives.

Strategy process research has emergence, politics, middle-manager discourse (Rouleau and Balogun, 2011; Wooldridge et al., 2008) but, as Hutzschenreuter and Kleindienst (2006) note, assumes a predefined decision object and cannot answer how issues come to be issues. Missing piece: attentional engagement.

Traditional ABV: limited issues at a time; what is attended moves forward; TMT attention often treated as organizational attention; structures send roles (analogy to Graen and Scandura dyadic organizing). Methodological individualism: collective attention reducible to individuals. Newer ABV (Joseph et al., 2024; Ocasio et al., 2018; Bartel and Rockmann, 2024; Tonellato et al., 2024) highlights relationships and emulation, but still mostly dyadic (actors’ relationships with objects, cues, signals). Bottom-up is often reframed as top managers attending to weak signals, not employees shaping attention processes (exception: Plotnikova et al., 2024).

### Dyadic vs triadic (Figure 1)

**Dyadic:** Actor A attending to object → individual action/behaviour → firm behaviour.

**Triadic:** Actor A eliciting B’s attention to a joint object; recursive attention (A attending to B’s attention); joint action → firm behaviour.

Triadic cognitive capability (Cook, 2018): handle relationships among three items at once. Example: developing an online ordering system, then “Sam, look, I’m developing…” and “Sam, this is Pam.” The third party can also attend, so joint attention is interactive and generative.

Joint attention: behavioural triangulation among agents (Shteynberg, 2018); triadic relation self–other–object (Siposova and Carpenter, 2019). Two triangulations:

- **Actor–actor–issue:** eliciting another’s attention (and effort) toward an issue. Social influence implicated but not theorized in strategy emergence and ABV.
- **Actor–actor–actor:** eliciting attention toward a third actor; structural formation of a coattending group. Largely absent from prior work. Needed to see social structures as *outcomes* of agency, not only influences on attention.

**Recursive attention:** mindful attention to another’s attention (“I notice that Sam is interested in the same system as Pam”). Often hypothetical/forward-looking (Gavetti and Levinthal, 2000): the other is not yet attending. Shapes whose attention one seeks, to what, and how.

**Boundary assumptions:**

1. Theory of mind / social-cognitive skill: imagine issues and actors from another’s perspective without assuming sameness. Hierarchical intentionality (Dunbar; Kinderman et al.): level 1 = my mental states; functional theory of mind needs at least two nested intentional states; healthy adults often reach level 4.
2. Autonomous agency across levels: members at all levels can influence others’ relationships and elicit attention (more plausible in post-Chandlerian contexts).

### Four underpinnings of the process model (Figure 2)

1. Attention begins individual but can trickle up through coattendee interaction. Triadic processing activates a “personal and plural and irreducibly collective” perspective (Shteynberg, 2018).
2. Emergent initiatives are products of joint attention in and across triads; they compete for TMT attention as adaptive real options.
3. Quality of attention matters more than quantity for initiative development (they explicitly align with the quality turn: stability, coherence, vividness via sustained interactive engagement). Joint engagement lets coattendees learn about one another and elaborate issues into initiatives.
4. Processing and agency are shaped by individual differences and placement in attention structures. Differences define conditions under which joint attention can emerge.

Three phases with feedback loops (later phases can reshape earlier engagement and structures).

### Phase 1: triadic agency in developing joint attention

Influencing others’ attention is natural; humans allow attention to be guided by others (Mundy and Newell, 2007). Two mechanisms: **brokering** (shape coattendee structures) and **rhetorical work** (shared meaning). Analytically distinct, inseparable in practice.

**Brokering.** Triad = ego + two alters. Two types:

- **Tertius gaudens (TG), “the third who enjoys”:** separate others; benefit from missing ties; control exchanges. Joint-attention version: not directing one alter’s attention toward another or toward a joint issue, or diverting existing attention. Can be self-interested or protect collective goals when certain interactions would undermine joint action (Lingo and O’Mahony, 2010).
- **Tertius iungens (TI), “the third who joins”:** unify; enable direct exchanges; elicit attention to one another and to mutual issues. Critical for innovation and coordination.

Both can occur in open or closed triads. Combining them is like throwing a dinner party (Obstfeld, 2017): invite those who make the best gathering (iungens), exclude those who do not fit (gaudens).

Alex–Ben–Celine–Dana example: Alex introduces Ben and Celine (TI) and elicits both to the issue; keeps Dana separate (TG) while maybe still extracting information. Exclusion guided by recursive attention (Dana lacks interest/knowledge/fit) is more productive than exclusion from dislike or rivalry.

**Proposition 1.** TI and TG brokering facilitate higher attentional engagement within the coattending group when based on high rather than low recursive attention to others’ attention (to issues and actors).

Joined actors can further reshape structures (Celine may occupy new structural holes and TI- or TG-broker Emma). Inclusion of TI-oriented actors → expanded denser group: more coherence/stability, more redundancy, less vividness. Inclusion of TG-oriented actors → vividness and diversity, weaker stability/coherence.

**Proposition 2.** Including both TI- and TG-oriented actors, rather than a group dominated by either, leads to higher attentional engagement.

Excluded actors’ orientations also matter. A TI-oriented Dana may still introduce valuable Frank; a TG-oriented Dana withholds.

**Proposition 3.** Excluding an actor with valuable contacts is less likely to hinder inclusion of relevant parties and their engagement when the excluded actor is more TI-oriented than TG-oriented.

**Rhetorical work.** Three functions: (1) issue-selling (elicit/engage attention to a joint issue); (2) motivating inclusion and justifying exclusion; (3) joint language.

Issue-selling (Dutton and Ashford, 1993) here is not only selling to seniors but initiating and sustaining joint engagement at early (underdeveloped ideas) and later (well-developed issues) stages. Knowledge articulation and discursive appeals to others’ interests.

Motivating inclusion aligns with TI (direct: get Celine to meet Ben; indirect: get Celine to introduce Emma). Justifying exclusion aligns with TG; needed because TG looks self-interested, especially when targeting low-power actors (Burt, 1997). Fairness/justification research (van Prooijen et al., 2004).

**Proposition 4.** When brokering is aligned with rhetorical work to (a) elicit attention to a strategic issue (issue-selling) and (b) influence relationships (motivating inclusion or justifying exclusion), attentional engagement within the coattending group is higher.

**Proposition 5.** (a) The higher the TG orientation of an included actor, the more motivating inclusion facilitates that actor’s engagement. (b) The higher the TI orientation of an excluded actor, the more effectively justifying exclusion still enables access to relevant others.

Joint language: new concepts and vocabularies for emerging issues (Jalonen et al., 2018; Ocasio et al., 2018) plus shared understandings of roles, routines, participation. Supports coordination, identity, cohesion, and later cognitive alignment and shared trust.

**Proposition 6.** By deepening connections to joint issues and other actors, joint language development supports sustained attentional engagement within the coattending group.

### Phase 2: emergence of collectively attended strategic initiatives

Target-match principle (Fulmer and Ostroff, 2016): explain group-level phenomena with group-level factors. Need a collective entity that attends (“we”), not just individuals attending to the group’s initiative. Two micro–macro mechanisms: cognitive alignment and shared trust.

**Cognitive alignment.** Coordinated interpretation oriented to implications for “us” not just “me.” Sustained interactive joint engagement produces it: recursive attending to how each coattendee engages; deeper processing from a collective standpoint; common knowledge and joint language; coalescence into “we think X about Y.” Not mere aggregation. Disagreement can remain (ambivalence, constructive competition, Joseph and Wilson, 2018; Healey et al., 2015) and add vividness; excessive value conflict produces incoherence/instability and feeds back to Phase 1 brokering/rhetoric. Alignment enables who-knows-what, transactive memory, collective mind, compensation, prediction, preemptive information (Cannon-Bowers and Salas, 2001).

**Proposition 7.** Emergence of cognitive alignment as a collective state enables collective-level attentional engagement with the strategic issue, thus championing it as a collective strategic initiative.

**Shared trust.** Willingness to be vulnerable (Mayer et al., 1995). Cognition-based (competence, engagement, dependability observed in how others attend) and affect-based (connection from joint attention, strong ties, joint language). Especially important for TG-oriented members seen as less dependable. Distrust can also be learned; continuing triadic agency is needed. Collective-level trust is not the sum of dyadic trusts: recursive observation of A trusting B spirals; identification-based generalized trust lets weakly connected members trust via belonging. Shared trust creates predictability so different parts of the group can attend interdependently; TG’s usual trust penalty weakens when the broker is seen as one of “us.”

**Proposition 8.** Emergence of shared trust as a collective state enables collective-level attentional engagement with the strategic issue, thus championing it as a collective strategic initiative.

### Phase 3: attentional engagement among competing strategic initiatives

To enter the firm agenda, the initiative must gain attention from decision makers not originally involved, especially TMT. Incremental brokering and issue-selling help, but TMTs distribute attention without personal vesting in everything. Need more than selling: relative advantage.

Collective “we” is glue: who we are (relationship structure) and what we do (the initiative). Strong coattention makes the initiative harder to ignore (coalitions as credibility; Ferris et al.; Ibarra et al.). Initiatives not prioritized even by their advocates will not attract managers.

**Proposition 9.** The stronger the collective-level attentional engagement with both the initiative and the coattention structure, the more likely coattendees are to prioritize the initiative over competing ones, making it harder for top management to ignore.

TMT engagement depends on three interrelated relationships: (1) how the initiative integrates with the firm (structures, goals, resources); (2) how the firm views relevant threats/opportunities; (3) how the initiative is believed to address them. Lack of relevance in any shifts attention away. Position the initiative as triangulation among firm, problem, and solution—not in isolation. Extends earlier work that treated initiatives as possible “solutions” in garbage-can terms.

**Proposition 10.** The more the coattending group directs top managers’ attention to problems relevant to the initiative, to the relevance of those problems and the solution for the firm, and to triangulation among these elements, the more likely the initiative is to gain sustained attentional engagement and become part of top management’s agenda.

Real-options logic (Barnett, 2008): initiatives as alternative directions. In turbulent/competitive environments, imminent threats/opportunities (digital media for newspapers; rise of AI) leave TMTs less discretion; diverse initiatives matter more. In stable/munificent environments, a smaller pool may suffice.

**Proposition 11.** The more turbulent and competitive the firm’s business environment, the more top managers narrow attention to salient threats or opportunities, thereby ignoring strategic initiatives not clearly linked to these concerns.

### Contributions, practice, future research

Three contributions:

1. Micro-social underpinnings of how organizational attention is shaped bottom-up. Alternative to hierarchical coherence/control (Ocasio et al., 2023): influence of an emergent collectively attending group. Clarifies embedded agency: actors are enabled and constrained by attention structures yet shape them. Advances the “we” as poorly understood aspect of organizational attention; collective-level attention differs qualitatively from individual attention; links to collective mind.
2. Triadic processing as part of ABV: not only vertical dependence (hierarchy) but lateral interdependence. Recursive attention as a new concept; sheds light on vividness (multiple attendees from different social worlds). Bridges social-cognitive joint-attention research and managerial ABV.
3. Missing attentional element in strategy-process emergence: how issues are constructed and become initiatives, linking sociopolitical work to construction of issues, initiatives, and coattending structures. Responds to Weiser et al. (2020) on structure and agency in strategy adaptation.

Practical implications by level:

- **Operational employees:** good ideas are rarely sufficient. Collectively interrogated, jointly developed initiatives carry social-movement momentum; individual voices become cacophony (Lingo, 2023). Champion who attends, who could be engaged, and how.
- **Middle managers:** not only react to cues from below; broker between operational employees and TMT. Recursive attention to which seniors can confer legitimacy and how they relate to issue and actors. Coordinate across attentional processes.
- **Top managers:** take bottom-up attention seriously as a source of otherwise unnoticed options. Distinguish genuine potential from vocal/powerful advocacy. Design forums where members can observe one another’s attention and autonomously shape relationship structures.

Future research: other mechanisms besides brokering/rhetoric/alignment/trust; when joint attention fails or initiatives become ephemeral; feasibility vs other adaptation modes (heuristics, external cues); costs and politicization; how joint attention interacts with top-down distribution; power and status (high-status captures attention more easily but can reduce sensitivity to others’ relationships and erode recursive-attention quality); triadic ABV as a leadership lens beyond hierarchy; multiple networks and TG actors splitting attention across groups; friendship ties compelling inclusion of unhelpful actors.

Conclusion: seminal ABV explained TMT distribution and enactment; less is known about bottom-up emergence because of the dyadic focus. A joint ABV outlines key mechanisms without claiming completeness.

## Cross-paper map: who agrees with whom, and where they actually disagree

### Shared ground across all four

- Ocasio (1997, 2011) is the reference definition and the idea of attentional engagement.
- Post-Chandlerian organizing (Ocasio et al., 2023) is the empirical motive: knowledge is dispersed; AI and digital media change attentional conditions; bottom-up strategy matters.
- Situatedness is underdeveloped in mainstream ABV (all four say this, in different vocabularies).
- Communication, interaction, and socio-material arrangements matter (Ocasio et al., 2018; Nicolini and Korica, 2021; Nicolini and Mengis, 2024; Splitter et al., 2024).
- Inattention, tradeoffs, and politics of attention are under-studied.

### The ontological fork (the real JMS fight)

| Question | Seidl et al. | Rhee | Nicolini et al. |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is attention? | Practices of attending; exists only as performance | Scarce, selective, situated mechanism | Still ABV attention, but competition-, situation-, and scaffolding-sensitive |
| Is scarcity constitutive? | No; crescive; limits can be overcome through practice/learning | Yes; tradeoff is the law; growth is reallocation | Limits exist; “scarcity” is a historically specific, not always apt, description |
| Homogeneous? | No; five constitutive qualities | Differentiation is real but not a new ontology | Quality and quantity mutually constitute each other |
| Role of practices | Constitutive of attention | Descriptive architectures/arenas | Complements; inhabited structures; competition among sources |
| Simon | Carnegie inheritance to supersede | Formulation, not source, of the constraint | Historical context to complete a turn away from |
| Method vs ontology | Ontological, not methodological | Agrees; rejects the quality/quantity *label* | Agrees dichotomies are unhelpful |
| Blind spots / missed disruption | Not the central explanandum | The test case the Point cannot pass | Inattention as agentic; politics of attention |
| Joint attention | Sociality of practices; cites Kauppila and Vaara | Not a focus | Thicker, interactional, Tomasello/Kidwell; not merely additive |
| Normative agenda | Secondary (competence, teloi); mainly descriptive qualities | Central: how attention ought to be allocated under constraint | Managerial: competition, blind spots, abundance via tech |

Kauppila and Vaara do not join the ontology fight as such. They still talk about scarce TMT attention, competing initiatives, and quality (stability, coherence, vividness) mattering more than quantity. They also treat attention as something that can be elicited, shared, and built into an irreducibly collective “we.” That is closer to Nicolini’s thicker joint attention and Seidl’s sociality than to Rhee’s insistence that collective engagement never produces more attention, only redistributes it. Their Phase 3, however, is Rhee-compatible: TMT attention remains a bottleneck amid competing issues.

### Concepts that travel across papers

- **Attentional engagement (Ocasio, 2011).** All use it. Seidl: still too cognitive/intentional. Rhee: already social without dropping scarcity. Nicolini: useful but overly intentional. Kauppila and Vaara: the dependent process to be built jointly.
- **Situated attention.** Seidl internalizes situation into practices. Rhee keeps situation as the bounded field from which attention exists, distinct from attention itself. Nicolini wants situations as agential units. Kauppila and Vaara: situations and network positions condition who can broker whom.
- **Crescive vs scarce.** The Point’s most contested word. Rhee: crescion is narrowing and silent on cost. Nicolini: capacity can be scaffolded and collectively extended without claiming limitlessness.
- **Quality.** Seidl: constitutive (five qualities), not evaluative. Rhee: differentiation already in ABV; do not confuse with crescion. Nicolini: the Point’s five qualities describe attending, not attention quality; quality and quantity interact. Kauppila and Vaara: quality ≈ Rerup’s stability/coherence/vividness via joint engagement.
- **Competition.** Rhee: issues and practices compete because of scarcity. Nicolini: biased competition as the replacement for allocation-control, including bottom-up salience and politics. Kauppila and Vaara: initiatives compete for TMT attention in Phase 3; TG/TI brokering is competition over who is in the coattending set.
- **Inattention / tradeoffs.** Rhee Agenda 2; Nicolini’s agentic inattention; Kauppila and Vaara’s exclusion (TG) and TMT ignoring of unlinked initiatives (P11).

### What a careful reader should not conflate

- Seidl’s “qualitative ABV” is not “use qualitative methods.” All three JMS papers agree on that, then fight about ontology.
- Seidl’s “quality” is not Weick and Sutcliffe’s “better attention.” They say so explicitly, then allow a secondary competence reading.
- Rhee’s scarcity is not “attention is a fixed pie that can never grow.” He allows deepening and capacity growth; he denies costless expansion.
- Nicolini’s “abundance” is not Seidl’s crescion. It is situational, collective, and socio-technically scaffolded expansion of *experienced* capacity, while denying limitlessness.
- Kauppila and Vaara’s “triadic” is not Seidl’s five qualities. It is a social-cognitive geometry (self, other, object) plus recursive attention, used to explain strategy emergence.
